1 Agentic AI

No NHS agent deployment met our sourcing standard this week, so this section carries one UK cyber security publication on agentic AI and one set of government guidance on AI in social care, and says where each stops.

Headline AI development

On 20 October the National Cyber Security Centre introduced a new chapter on AI in its research problem book. The NCSC says generative and agentic AI systems can plan, reason, use tools and take actions with increasing autonomy, which makes some tasks much easier to automate and creates new problems to solve. The chapter sets out seven areas across three broad themes, and the NCSC says the common thread is human and AI collaboration: understanding where autonomy can provide genuine value, where human judgement remains essential, and how meaningful oversight can be kept without losing the speed of automation. The publication is a set of research questions, and the NCSC invites feedback and examples of ongoing or planned research. It follows the NCSC item on AI agents in defence that we covered in the 6 to 12 October issue.

Source: NCSC, Introducing a new AI chapter in the NCSC research problem book
Healthcare or NHS use case

On 21 October HTN reported DHSC guidance on using AI in adult social care, which GOV.UK shows was published on 29 September. The guidance says AI care planning software may cut down the time spent on administrative tasks, which can allow care staff more time to give people care. It lists meeting notes and action lists, recruitment administration and the auditing of care records among other administrative uses. It is guidance for adult social care providers in England and covers AI in general, so it is not specific to agents, and it says many providers are still at an early stage of exploring AI.

Source: GOV.UK, Using AI in adult social care · HTN, DHSC guidance on digitising adult social care, managing IT suppliers, using AI
Governance or ethics note

The DHSC guidance says that if AI is used in care planning, a member of care staff must review the plan to make sure it is accurate, and that a human should review anything AI produces. It advises providers to define what they hope to achieve before choosing any technology, to define the human role within any use of AI, and to write an AI policy. The NCSC says many assumptions behind today's cyber security practice, including vulnerability management, incident response and risk management, were developed before this kind of AI existed and will need to evolve. Neither source tells an NHS organisation how to govern a clinical agent. A practical reading, which is uPull.ai's and not either source's, is that a team trialling an agent should know what it can do without a person, who can stop it, and where its actions are recorded.

Source: GOV.UK, Using AI in adult social care · NCSC, Introducing a new AI chapter in the NCSC research problem book
Try this in a workflow

Choose one automated or agent based tool that your organisation uses or is considering. On one page, write down what it can do without a person, who can stop it, where its actions are logged, and how many minutes it is expected to save. Share the page with your cyber security or information governance lead this week and note any question you cannot answer.

3 Ambient AI

One trust account this week, with a patient benefit. It comes from the trust's own news story as reported by HTN, and it carries no time saving figures.

Healthcare or NHS use case

The Christie NHS Foundation Trust has shared how it is using ambient voice technology to support patient experience. At its Macclesfield site, a consultant haematologist used the technology in clinic for the first time, and the patient received the post consultation summary almost immediately, before leaving. The patient told the trust that normally the letter arrives a week or so later and that having it straight away meant everything was fresh in their mind. The trust says clinicians remain responsible for checking, editing and approving all correspondence before it is sent to patients or GPs. HTN reports that the trust began a phased roll out in surgery and haematology in January 2026 and plans to extend to multidisciplinary team meetings, board rounds and virtual clinics. The consultant said the technology can significantly reduce time spent on clinical documentation. The reports give no figure for the time saved.

Source: The Christie NHS Foundation Trust, Disley patient benefits from faster clinical review letter thanks to new technology · HTN, The Christie shares impact of AVT on patient experience
Governance or ethics note

The safeguard the trust describes is that the clinician checks, edits and approves every letter before it is sent. The reports do not say how often letters are edited or how the trust is measuring accuracy, and the benefit described rests on one patient's account and one clinician's view. In September we covered a University of Edinburgh review of what ambient scribes may leave out of the record, such as tone and hesitation. A same day letter is only as good as the check behind it. Whether a fast letter is also a complete one is a fair question for any trust, and it is uPull.ai's question, not the trust's.

Source: The Christie NHS Foundation Trust, Disley patient benefits from faster clinical review letter thanks to new technology · HTN, The Christie shares impact of AVT on patient experience
Try this in a workflow

Pick one type of clinic letter your service sends. Record how many days it currently takes from appointment to letter, and who reads it before it goes out. If you run or plan an ambient voice pilot, measure the same two things before and after, sample 20 letters for edits, and write down what the freed time was used for.

4 Clinical AI

The regulator describes its move from strategy to practice, a UK research partnership shows what trusts can build together, and one deadline falls this week.

Headline AI development

On 20 October the MHRA's Deputy Director for AI and Software was due to speak to US industry leaders at the AdvaMed 2026 conference, and the MHRA published its outline on 19 October. The release says the MHRA will set out its developing approach to software and AI enabled medical devices, including more flexible lifecycle regulation, regulatory sandboxes and closer international collaboration. It follows the MHRA 2030 Strategy, which we covered last week. The release quotes the Deputy Director: “In the UK we are moving from policy into practice, using initiatives like AI Airlock to build a more proportionate, lifecycle-based approach.” It also names new regional partnerships with NHS London and Manchester University NHS Foundation Trust to test technologies in real health settings and generate evidence for future regulation.

Source: GOV.UK, UK regulator brings new AI and MedTech agenda to AdvaMed
Healthcare or NHS use case

PharosAI, a partnership between Queen Mary University of London, King's College London, Barts Health and Guy's and St Thomas', has received Innovate UK funding for a project called DESTINY. On 15 October the university reported that the project will test whether AI can predict molecular information from routinely collected tissue data that would otherwise need costly and time consuming tests, with the longer term aim of building digital twins of human tissue. It will use federated learning, which lets models learn from data held by different organisations without moving the data into one place. The funding supports an initial feasibility study, and successful projects can apply for up to £10 million of further funding.

Source: Queen Mary University of London, PharosAI awarded funding to explore AI digital twins of human tissue
Governance or ethics note

Two limits apply. DESTINY is a feasibility study with no results yet, and the university says federated learning on complex combinations of biological and medical data is less explored than other approaches. On regulation, the Government's Health Bill amendments on medical devices, which the MHRA announced on 1 September, state that the proposals do not introduce immediate changes to the regulation of medicines or medical devices. The direction is clear. Nothing in the sources we read changes what a deploying organisation must do today.

Source: Queen Mary University of London, PharosAI awarded funding to explore AI digital twins of human tissue · GOV.UK, MHRA regulatory reform amendments tabled in Government's Health Bill
Date to note

The MHRA's AI Airlock Phase 3 application page says the application window stays open year round. For an application to be considered in the current review wave, the page asks applicants to apply before 31 October, and it says a later shortlisting period will take place in Spring 2027. There is no application or participation fee. The page says candidates are currently expected to fund their own studies and delivery of any Airlock testing. This is a summary for information, not advice on whether to apply.

Source: GOV.UK, AI Airlock Phase 3 application
Try this in a workflow

Ask each supplier of an AI enabled tool what evidence they will generate after go live and how they will share it with your organisation. Record the answer alongside the supplier's clinical safety documentation, so that the question of who owns monitoring has a named answer before the tool is switched on.

5 Learning & Development

Two ways to build skills this week: an open clinical AI fellowship that closes on 16 November, and free government guidance that gives care teams a structure for adopting AI.

NHS Fellowship in Clinical AI, Cohort 6

A fellowship for health and care professionals who want to learn the safe deployment and evaluation of AI in clinical workflows.

Who it's for
There are two kinds of applicant. Competitive applicants apply for a post already attached to funding, if they meet the eligibility criteria, and salary cover is provided in the funded posts. Nominated applicants are nominated for a post with ringfenced funding and must identify a sponsor or fund their own post.
How to access
Both routes use the same application form. Every applicant needs an Approval in Principle from their Approver, submitted on a separate form by the same deadline, and applicants will not be interviewed without it. The page also links a recording of the Cohort 6 applicant webinar.
Cost and time
The course fee is £8,000, met by a sponsor or by the applicant, and fellows must be released at 0.4 FTE for the 12 months of the fellowship. The page says there is no salary cover unless agreed with the sponsor, and that Cohort 5 had 310 eligible applicants for 34 competitively allocated posts.
What to expect
Shortlisting outcomes and interview invitations go out on 11 December 2026. Remote interviews run between 13 and 29 January 2027, outcomes and project matching follow in February 2027, and Cohort 6 starts on 11 August 2027.
Deadline
Applications and Approval in Principle forms close at 23:45 GMT on 16 November 2026.
Source: NHS Fellowship in Clinical AI, apply page (page read on 26 October 2026)

Using AI in adult social care, DHSC guidance

Free GOV.UK guidance for adult social care providers on where AI is used today, how to decide whether it is the right answer, and how to introduce it with human review.

Who it's for
Adult social care providers and managers in England. NHS teams who work alongside social care partners will find the questions useful for shared pathways.
How to access
The guidance is free on GOV.UK. It sits in the Digitising adult social care collection, which also holds guidance on digital champions, choosing and managing IT suppliers, and a digital skills framework.
What it covers
Uses of AI in care planning, monitoring and administration. Best practice on data quality, ethics and data protection, workforce skills and human review, including defining what you hope to achieve before choosing technology, defining the human role, and writing an AI policy.
Deadline
None. The guidance was published on 29 September 2026.
Source: GOV.UK, Using AI in adult social care · GOV.UK, Digitising adult social care
