Authority Has to Be Earned

Last week's issue showed the government putting dates on the regulatory programme for healthcare AI. This week the regulator's own direction arrives. The MHRA published its Strategy 2030 on 14 October, promising an adaptive framework for AI as a medical device and a new Centre for AI and Digital Health, though most of its AI commitments carry no delivery date. Guy's and St Thomas' reports the UK's first operation with a robot that lets the surgeon feel resistance. An ambulance service is testing AI note-taking with a clinician editing every note, and the American Medical Association asks who is accountable when an AI agent takes on a clinical function. Each story turns on the same question: what earns an AI tool its authority, and who holds the evidence?

1 Agentic AI

No new NHS agent deployment met our sourcing standard this week, so this section carries one governance framework from the United States and says where it stops.

Headline AI development

The American Medical Association published a governance framework on 13 October for agentic AI systems that take on clinical functions. Its core principle is that clinical authority is earned through evidence and kept through demonstrated performance, and that authority and oversight can change as evidence, risk or the technology changes. The framework organises its thinking around four questions: what clinical function the AI performs, what shows it can perform that function and what happens if it fails, what it should be permitted to do and under what conditions, and who is responsible for the risks and outcomes. MedTech Dive reports that the framework covers uses such as ambient documentation, diagnostics and patient monitoring, and that the AMA will work with physicians and with the people who build, deploy and regulate AI on liability, licensure and regulation. The AMA page names no requirements and assigns responsibility to no party. It is the position of a US professional body, and it is neither UK regulation nor NHS policy.

Source: AMA, Governance framework for agentic AI assuming clinical functions · MedTech Dive, AMA releases governance framework for agentic AI in healthcare
Governance or ethics note

MedTech Dive notes that agentic systems can complete multi-step tasks without a human in the loop, so an error could cause harm before a clinician steps in. The AMA's fourth question, who is responsible, is posed and left open. For a UK organisation the question has a practical shape: the supplier, the deploying organisation and the individual user each hold part of the answer, and the framework does not apportion it. That reading is uPull.ai's, and the source does not make it.

Source: MedTech Dive, AMA releases governance framework for agentic AI in healthcare
Try this in a workflow

Choose one automated or agent-based tool that your organisation uses or is considering. Answer the AMA's four questions about it on a single page: its function, the evidence behind it and what happens if it fails, what it may do and under what conditions, and who is responsible. Share the page with your clinical safety officer or information governance lead this week and note any question you cannot answer.

2 Robotics AI

A UK first in the operating theatre, reported with one patient's account and no outcome data yet.

Headline AI development

Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust reports that its surgeons have begun using Force Feedback instruments with the da Vinci 5 robotic system, supplied by Intuitive. The trust's own page says the first operation took place on 13 October, a hiatus hernia repair carried out by Professor James Gossage, consultant upper gastrointestinal surgeon. Until now the surgeon at the console relied on vision. With the new instruments the hand controls give a sense of resistance, so the surgeon can feel the pressure applied to tissue. Channel 4 News, which described it as the UK's first operation of its kind, reports that the force sensors measure push, pull and direction about 1,000 times a second. The trust says the instruments are also in use for colorectal, gynaecological and upper gastrointestinal surgery. The trust describes them as newly approved but does not name the regulator or the mark.

Source: Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, New robotic technology gives surgeons a sense of touch in UK first · Channel 4 News, Revealed: UK's first operation using a robot that can feel
Healthcare or NHS use case

The trust gives the setting for the first cases: 13 surgical robots and three diagnostic robots across five hospitals, a surgical fleet that includes da Vinci, Hugo and Versius systems alongside an orthopaedic knee robot, and more than 17,000 patients who have had robotic-assisted surgery at the trust. It reports that colorectal patients go home about four days sooner on average than after open surgery. That figure describes robotic surgery in general at the trust and is not a result for the new instruments. The first patient says they are recovering faster than expected.

Source: Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, New robotic technology gives surgeons a sense of touch in UK first
Governance or ethics note

The evidence offered for the new instruments is one patient's experience and a reference to promising early US results, with no study cited and no outcome figures. The trust page does not mention training for surgeons using the new instruments. Channel 4 News makes the same point through the patient, who compares owning the technology without knowing how to use it to having a Ferrari without knowing how to drive. For a trust considering the same upgrade, three questions follow: which approval covers the instruments, what evidence the supplier can show, and how the first cases are recorded and reviewed. Those questions are uPull.ai's own, and neither source answers them.

Source: Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, New robotic technology gives surgeons a sense of touch in UK first · Channel 4 News, Revealed: UK's first operation using a robot that can feel
Try this in a workflow

If your trust runs a robotic surgery programme, ask the programme lead for a one page record of how each new instrument or software release is introduced. It should name who approves it, what evidence they reviewed, and how the first cases are logged and reviewed afterwards.

3 Ambient AI

One ambulance trial this week. We found no other ambient item that met our sourcing standard, and the source for this one is a republished report, which we flag below.

Healthcare or NHS use case

North East Ambulance Service is running an eight-week trial of ambient voice software with 38 staff, from paramedics to operational staff. The software listens to conversations between crews and patients, transcribes them and writes a summary. It runs on the microphones of the iPads that crews already carry, so no new hardware is needed, and it is being tested in live patient encounters. The service was halfway through at the time of reporting, the trial ends in November, and the service will then decide its next steps. NEAS says it wants to learn whether the tool reduces time spent on paperwork and whether it makes records more consistent and accurate. Georgina Campbell, the service's digital clinical lead, said the aim is to use the technology safely and effectively without lowering care or record keeping standards. The report names no supplier and gives no early results. We read this through Resultsense, which republishes a BBC News report dated 13 October. The BBC page could not be opened from our systems, and the NEAS press page lists no release on the trial.

Source: Resultsense, NEAS paramedics AI note-taking trial (republishing BBC News)
Governance or ethics note

The reported safeguard is that a clinician must read and edit every AI-generated note before it is filed to a patient record. The report does not say how the trial will compare the AI summaries with the clinicians' edits, so it is unclear whether the edit rate will be measured. It also does not mention patient consent or how patients are told that a recording is made. Emergency care adds its own conditions: noise, distress and urgency. Whether those conditions affect transcription accuracy is a fair question for the evaluation, and it is uPull.ai's question, not the source's.

Source: Resultsense, NEAS paramedics AI note-taking trial (republishing BBC News)
Try this in a workflow

If you run or plan an ambient voice pilot, sample 20 notes and record for each one whether the clinician edited it and what kind of edit it was: omission, wrong detail or wording. Agree with your clinical safety officer what edit rate would make you pause the pilot.

4 Clinical AI

The regulator publishes its direction to 2030, one week after the government set its own dates.

Headline AI development

On 14 October the MHRA published its Strategy 2030, introduced by chief executive Lawrence Tallon. For AI, the GOV.UK announcement commits the agency to a modern, adaptive regulatory framework for AI as a medical device, a new MHRA Centre for AI and Digital Health to clarify expectations for AI-driven devices and medicines, and an AI Medicines Development Action Plan in 2027. It also commits to using AI to analyse real-world data so that safety issues are flagged earlier. The executive summary lists ten strategic priorities, including enabling technology and AI, and setting the global standard for medtech and AI. The only AI date on the GOV.UK pages we read is 2027 for the medicines plan, and the AI device framework has no stated delivery date. Last week's issue reported the government's dated steps, including draft MHRA guidance by December 2026 on changes to AI enabled devices. This strategy sets the longer horizon.

Source: GOV.UK, MHRA 2030 strategy to modernise patient centred regulation for the era of AI and personalised medicines · GOV.UK, MHRA Strategy 2030 executive summary
Healthcare or NHS use case

HTN's report of 15 October lists further commitments that would reach NHS organisations buying and running AI devices. They are to expand Predetermined Change Control Plans so that pre-agreed model updates can be made without unnecessary delay, to explore master files for foundation models, to make unique device identifiers compulsory through new pre-market regulations so devices can be traced across their lifecycle, to strengthen post-market surveillance in real-world use, to offer conditional approval for innovative technologies through the Early Access Service for Devices, and to support legislative reform for statutory regulatory sandboxes. HTN gives no delivery dates for these. The GOV.UK announcement adds simpler Yellow Card reporting and closer work on real-world surveillance with the Health Data Research Service.

Source: HTN, MHRA 2030 strategy vows to reduce regulatory barriers and create modern framework for AI as a medical device · GOV.UK, MHRA 2030 strategy announcement
Governance or ethics note

A strategy states intent, and nothing in the sources we read changes what a deploying organisation must do today. Statutory sandboxes depend on legislative reform, and most AI device commitments are undated. The practical value for a trust is in the direction: traceability of devices, controlled model updates and continuous monitoring all point towards more evidence being expected from suppliers and from deployers after go live.

Source: HTN, MHRA 2030 strategy vows to reduce regulatory barriers and create modern framework for AI as a medical device
Try this in a workflow

List the AI enabled tools in your organisation that may be medical devices. For each, ask the supplier two questions and record the answers: can it identify the device by a unique identifier, and how does it tell you when the model has been updated? Compare the answers with the strategy's direction on traceability and controlled updates.

5 Learning & Development

Three ways to build skills this month: an open clinical AI fellowship, expanded robotic surgery training for surgical trainees, and a clinical risk management course taking expressions of interest for 2027.

NHS Fellowship in Clinical AI, Cohort 6

A 12 month programme at 0.4 FTE, which is two days or 15 hours a week, for health and care professionals who want to learn the safe deployment and evaluation of AI in clinical workflows.

Who it's for
Registered health or social care professionals working for an NHS employer apply by the competitive route. Health and social care staff providing NHS services can apply by the nominated route, with a sponsor or self funding. Doctors and dentists on the competitive route need a National Training Number and a projected CCT date after 10 August 2028.
How to access
Both routes use one application form, and your Approver must submit an Approval in Principle form by the same deadline. The nominated route carries an £8,000 course fee and no salary cover unless the sponsor agrees to provide it.
What it covers
Fellows join existing NHS clinical AI projects. The apply page reports that Cohort 5 had 310 eligible applicants for 34 competitively allocated posts.
Deadline
Applications close at 23:45 GMT on 16 November 2026. Remote interviews are expected between 13 and 29 January 2027, and Cohort 6 starts on 11 August 2027.
Source: NHS Fellowship in Clinical AI, apply page (last updated 13 October 2026)

START robotic surgery training, expanding in 2026/27

Surgical Training in Advanced Robotic Technology is an accredited, platform-agnostic training programme for surgical trainees, developed by the Shelford Group with the Newcastle Surgical Training Centre and supported by the Association of Surgeons in Training.

Who it's for
Surgical trainees. The programme already runs in the North East, North West and East of England.
How to access
From the 2026/27 academic year it also runs in Thames Valley, Wessex, and Kent, Surrey and Sussex, which brings it to six of England's thirteen deanery footprints. Oxford University Hospitals leads delivery in Thames Valley and Wessex, and the Minimal Access Therapy Training Unit at Royal Surrey County Hospital delivers in Kent, Surrey and Sussex. The page we reviewed does not describe an application route, so trainees should ask their regional training programme.
What it covers
Training across the da Vinci, Versius and Hugo systems over 36 to 48 months, combining e-learning, industry-led technology training, simulation, hands-on skills days and supervised clinical experience, with an online skills tracker. The Royal College of Surgeons of England accredited it in March 2026.
Deadline
No application date is stated. The expansion begins in the 2026/27 academic year.
Source: Oxford University Hospitals, Expansion of pioneering robotic surgery training programme announced (published 11 August 2026)

Clinical Risk Management course, IPEM and NHS England

A remotely delivered course on clinical risk management for health IT systems, run with the NHS England Digital Clinical Informatics Safety Team. It is relevant to every category in this issue because each depends on a named clinical safety owner.

Who it's for
Clinical scientists, engineers and technologists involved in clinical software development, IT system upgrades or implementation, or the governance of clinical IT systems. The course is open to NHS employees.
How to access
Booking is not yet open. To be notified, email training@ipem.ac.uk. Before the course day you complete the e-Learning for Healthcare Digital Clinical Safety Training, Module 2 Intermediate, which is free for NHS employees, and submit your certificate.
What it covers
Ten pre-recorded modules, a training day with practical workshops and a one-hour round table. It covers DCB0129, on manufacturing health IT, and DCB0160, on deploying and using it.
Deadline
No date is listed. The IPEM page says the course is being planned for 2027.
Source: IPEM, Clinical Risk Management Course
