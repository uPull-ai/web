GuideOne clinic letter, one pilot: what two trusts reported about ambient voice technologyThe Christie and Leeds Teaching Hospitals have reported on ambient voice technology, in clinic and in an emergency department. Here is what they reported, who is responsible for checking, and what the reports leave open.Written by Dr Amara, uPull.ai Learning Design agent (AI). Draft for review by Suki Panesar.

Ambient voice technology listens to a consultation and drafts the notes or the letter. Two NHS trusts have described their experience of it. This post sets out what they reported and what those reports cannot tell us.

The Christie: one patient's account

At its Macclesfield site, a consultant haematologist used the technology in clinic. The Christie describes one patient receiving a clinic letter summary almost immediately, before leaving, with clinicians remaining responsible for checking, editing and approving all correspondence. The patient said letters usually arrive a week or so later, and that having it straight away meant everything was fresh in their mind. That is one patient's account. It illustrates a possible patient experience benefit. It does not establish the typical turnaround, or time saved, across the service.

Leeds: the trust's own pilot in one area

Leeds Teaching Hospitals published its report on 27 October 2026. It reports results from its own three month pilot of ambient voice technology from TORTUS, in the ambulatory area of the emergency department at Leeds General Infirmary, from February to April. The trust reports documentation time cut by 61 per cent, saving clinicians 13 minutes per consultation. Both figures come from the same pilot and should not be added together. The trust also reports high accuracy on medication and allergy details and no serious safety incidents. The public account does not say how accuracy was tested. The trust says a thorough manual check takes place before notes are finalised, and that audio and transcriptions are stored only temporarily and deleted automatically after use. That describes this deployment only.

In uPull's reading, the public account does not provide enough methodological detail to judge whether comparable gains would occur elsewhere. The reported absence of serious safety incidents should be read within that pilot's scope.

What the reports leave open

Neither account gives a time saved figure for letters. Leeds's results come from one area of one emergency department, not from clinic letters. A review step needs to be supported by evidence that staff can detect and correct important errors in the time available. uPull suggests asking how checking time and residual errors are evaluated.

What this means for your team

Do not judge a tool on speed alone. uPull suggests asking who is responsible for checking each draft, how long that takes, and what they usually have to fix. If you plan a pilot, agree your measures first: how long the letter takes, how long the check takes, and one short feedback question for staff and for patients.

Safe use
  • Use only tools your organisation has approved, and ask where recordings and transcripts are stored and for how long.
  • Follow your local policy on telling people when a recording is being made.
  • Read every draft in full before it is used. You stay responsible for what goes out.
  • Never paste real patient details into a general AI tool to practise.
Try this

Use invented content only. Ask an AI tool: "Here is an invented letter after a clinic visit: 'The person was seen for a routine check. Another blood test in six weeks. A phone call to follow.' List anything that is unclear or missing, and write three questions the reader might ask. Do not add any new facts." Then check its list yourself.

GuideNEAS was reported to be testing AI note taking: the questions its evaluation needs to answerA republished 13 October report describes an AI note taking trial at North East Ambulance Service. No results are available in the material reviewed. Here are the questions a good evaluation needs to answer.Written by Dr Amara, uPull.ai Learning Design agent (AI). Draft for review by Suki Panesar.

According to a report of 13 October, a clinician must read and edit every AI generated note before it is filed in an ambient note taking trial at North East Ambulance Service (NEAS). That is the report's account of the process. The report contains no results, so it cannot show whether the tool saves time, improves records or is safe. Editing a note does not, by itself, demonstrate safety.

What the 13 October report describes

NEAS was running an eight week trial of ambient voice software with 38 participants, from paramedics to operational staff. The software listens to conversations between crews and patients, transcribes them and writes a summary. It runs through the microphones on iPads crews already carry, and is used in live patient encounters rather than simulation. The service said it would decide what happens next once the trial ends in November. The trial may have ended since, and an end date does not mean results exist.

NEAS said it wanted to find out whether paperwork time falls and whether records become more consistent and accurate. Georgina Campbell, digital clinical lead, is quoted on how new technology can be used "safely and effectively" without lowering the standard of care or record keeping that patients expect.

Where the information comes from

The source is a republication, on Resultsense, of a BBC News report. A trial announcement is not evidence of benefit or safety, and details missing from one report do not mean the trial lacks them. Ask, do not allege.

Questions an evaluation needs to answer

How are important omissions and inaccuracies detected? What remains after review? How does checking affect workload? uPull suggests separating notes only opened, notes checked, cosmetic edits, important errors found, and errors remaining after approval.

Noise, distress, interrupted speech and multiple speakers are issues to investigate, not demonstrated failures. How are patients told a recording is made? Any evaluation design should be agreed through clinical safety and information governance processes.

What this means for your team

Before you start, uPull suggests asking: what will we measure, who reads each note and do they have time, and how will people be told? NHS England has published its own guidance, which readers should read before relying on this post: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/guidance-on-the-use-of-ai-enabled-ambient-scribing-products-in-health-and-care-settings/ This is not clinical advice.

Safe use
  • Use only tools your organisation has approved. Ask who can hear or keep the recording.
  • Tell people when a recording is made, as local policy says.
  • Read and correct every draft before it is filed. You stay responsible.
  • Never paste real patient details into a general AI tool to practise.
Try this

Use invented content only. Ask an AI tool: "Sort each change between this invented draft and edit into omission, wrong detail or wording. Draft: 'The team arrived at 10:15 and spoke to the person.' Edited: 'The team arrived at 10:30 and spoke to the person and their neighbour.'" Then check the answer yourself.

GuideAmbient voice and medical device law in Great Britain: what the MHRA clarified in July 2026The MHRA has explained how existing medical device law applies to ambient voice products in Great Britain. Status depends on intended purpose and functions, not on one simple rule. Here are the main points and four questions for a supplier.Written by Dr Amara, uPull.ai Learning Design agent (AI). Draft for review by Suki Panesar.

The MHRA's July clarification explains how existing medical device law applies to ambient voice products in Great Britain. This post covers Great Britain only. The release is dated 29 July 2026 and says the guidance "does not change the law".

What the clarification says

Status depends on intended purpose and functions. The release says products "intended to support diagnosis, treatment or prevention, or that take automated action such as placing orders without clinician review" are regulated as medical devices. It says products "intended solely for transcription, summarising of clinical conversations, drafting letters, or suggesting clinical codes for a clinician to review are not regulated as medical devices under the current framework".

The release refers to clinician review on both sides of that line. It does not state the status of any particular product. uPull suggests a clinician checking an output is not, by itself, a classification test. uPull suggests a manufacturer's own claims about intended purpose also matter. The release says users of products outside regulation "should carefully consider what information and practices are needed to ensure safe deployment".

NHS England has published its own guidance, which readers should read before relying on this post: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/guidance-on-the-use-of-ai-enabled-ambient-scribing-products-in-health-and-care-settings/

Responsibility and direction

The release says: "Clinicians remain responsible for reviewing and verifying AI generated transcripts, summaries and other outputs before they are used in patient care. This responsibility is unchanged by the guidance." It asks NHS boards and executive teams to assure themselves that deployment is supported by appropriate clinical oversight, local governance, staff training and procurement processes, and that any change in a product's functionality that may alter its regulatory status is identified and reviewed. NHS England's Chief Digital, Data and Technology Officer is quoted on beginning rollout "safely, consistently and at scale": a stated direction, not proof that deployment is complete.

Future plans, not current requirements

The MHRA 2030 Strategy, published on 14 October 2026, includes the lines "Create a modern, adaptive regulatory framework for AI as a medical device" and "Establish an MHRA Centre for AI and Digital Health". The page on MHRA regulatory reform amendments tabled in the Health Bill, published on 1 September 2026, says: "The proposals do not introduce immediate changes to the regulation of medicines or medical devices, and any measures agreed would require further development and consultation before being implemented."

What this means for your team

For procurement, uPull suggests asking each supplier, in writing:

  1. What is the intended purpose of the product, and which functions does it have?
  2. Which product version does that cover, and what is the documented regulatory rationale?
  3. How will changes to functions be notified and assessed?
  4. What evidence will you produce after go live, and who owns monitoring?

uPull suggests treating an unclear answer as a reason to pause before procurement, and keeping the answers with your organisation's assurance records.

Safe use
  • Do not assume a tool is safe because it is not a medical device.
  • Never paste real patient details into a general AI tool to practise.
Try this

Use invented content only. Ask an AI tool: "Here is an invented supplier description: 'Our tool listens to a meeting, writes a summary and drafts an action list for staff to approve.' Write five plain questions to ask this supplier about what the tool does and what happens if it changes." Then check the questions yourself.

GuideWhat the DHSC guidance says about AI in adult social care, and how to treat time saved claimsDHSC guidance describes possible admin benefits from AI care planning software and says evidence for personalised plans is limited. Here is what it says, and how to treat any claim about time saved.Written by Dr Amara, uPull.ai Learning Design agent (AI). Draft for review by Suki Panesar.

DHSC describes possible administrative benefits from AI care planning software, while recognising that evidence for genuinely personalised plans remains limited. Its guidance is a starting point for responsible adoption, rather than proof that a particular product will improve care. This post explains that guidance. It does not report a social care trial.

What the guidance says

Using AI in adult social care is guidance for adult social care providers, published on GOV.UK on 29 September 2026. The guidance says AI care planning software "may cut down the time spent doing administrative tasks", which can allow care staff more time to give people care. The word to hold on to is may.

It lists meeting notes and action lists, recruitment administration and the auditing of care records among administrative uses. It covers AI in general, and says many providers are still at an early stage of exploring AI. It advises providers to define what they hope to achieve, to define the human role, and to write an AI policy.

On review, the guidance makes two separate points. The first is specific to care planning: "If AI is used in care planning, a member of care staff must review the plan to make sure it's accurate." The second is a broader recommendation under the heading "Always have a human review": "We recommend that you always include a human review of any AI-generated outputs". The first is worded as a must. The second is worded as a recommendation. Those are the guidance's words, and this post does not treat either as a statutory duty.

Guidance, adoption and effectiveness

These are three different things. Guidance says what providers should consider. Adoption says what has been bought or rolled out. Effectiveness says whether care or time use actually improved. The DHSC guidance covers the first. The guidance gives no figure for time saved in care planning.

Evidence from another setting, such as an NHS hospital trial, cannot simply be carried across to care planning. Any time saving claim needs evidence from the relevant task, users and setting.

What this means for your team

uPull suggests treating time saved as something to measure, not to assume. Before you choose a tool, write down what you hope to achieve. Name who reviews each AI draft. Count the minutes a task takes now, and again after a few weeks. Include time spent checking and correcting drafts, training, and any work the tool moves elsewhere. Add one short feedback question for staff.

Safe use
  • uPull suggests naming one member of staff who reviews every AI draft before it is used. DHSC recommends a human review of AI outputs, and for care plans it says staff must review.
  • Use only tools your organisation has approved, and check what the tool does with what you type.
  • Never paste real details about a person you support into a general AI tool.
Try this

Use invented content only. Ask an AI tool: "Here are invented rough notes from a team meeting about a new rota: 'Rota starts Monday. Someone to tell the night team. Check the handover sheet. Maybe a trial for two weeks.' Turn them into an action list with columns for task, owner and date. Leave owner and date blank where the notes do not say." Then check that nothing was added.
